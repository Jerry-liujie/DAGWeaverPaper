
\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate \tool{} on real dbt projects, organized around three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How does \tool{} compare to existing baselines for SQL pipeline optimization?
    \item Where do \tool{}'s gains come from? We decompose them along the three optimization dimensions (refactoring, materialization, and frequency), and along the dataflow-driven targeting that feeds the LLM stage.
    \item Is the underlying machinery sound and affordable? We measure the accuracy of the cost model that drives materialization decisions, and the LLM cost of running the system end-to-end.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval-setup}

\head{Workloads}
We evaluate on two real dbt projects. \emph{Tuva} is an open-source dbt package for healthcare data transformation. The full project comprises 842 SQL models organized across 17 modules. We use the claims preprocessing module, which contains 255 models (about 30\% of the project) and is the subset whose models exhibit substantial computational complexity. We run it on a synthetic patient population of 10K, with the largest source table at 505\,MB. \emph{Stripe} is a payments-analytics project of 65 models, already factored by hand into intermediate stages so that the easy syntactic wins have been taken. We run it with the largest source table at 199\,MB. The two projects together cover the range we want to evaluate: a large project that has accumulated cross-model patterns from multiple analysts, and a smaller, already-factored project where remaining gains must come from materialization, frequency, or semantic restructuring beyond syntactic deduplication.

\head{Testbed}
Experiments run on BigQuery with a 100-slot reservation on the Standard edition under capacity-based pricing. We report slot-time as the primary cost metric, since slot-time is the billing unit under capacity pricing, and elapsed time as a secondary latency metric. Each reported number is the median of three runs. To simulate realistic production schedules, we use three frequency profiles per project that assign root and leaf models to hourly, daily, or weekly cadences, with interior models derived by propagation. \head{Cost metrics}
We report two views of cost. \emph{Single-run cost} is the cost of executing the full DAG once. \emph{Frequency-weighted cost} weights each model's per-execution cost by the rate at which it actually needs to run, summed over the DAG; under capacity pricing this corresponds to the project's billed cost over a period. Refactoring and materialization affect both metrics; frequency optimization affects only the frequency-weighted metric. Improvements are reported as percent reduction relative to the original project.

\head{Baselines}
We compare against three baselines spanning the design space of SQL pipeline optimization.

\emph{LearnedRewrite}~\cite{learnedrewrite} is a rule-based per-query rewriter. It applies a portfolio of equivalence-preserving transformations to each model's compiled SQL independently, guided by a learned policy that selects which rules to apply and in what order. It is competitive on standalone analytical queries but has no view of the surrounding DAG.

\emph{GenRewrite}~\cite{genrewrite} is an LLM-based per-query rewriter. It introduces natural-language rewrite rules that transfer knowledge across queries and a counterexample-guided loop that iteratively corrects syntactic and semantic errors in the rewrites. Like LearnedRewrite, it operates one model at a time.

\emph{CSE} is a cross-model common subexpression exploitation baseline patterned after Silva-Larson-Zhou~\cite{silva2012cse}, which detects subexpressions shared across a SCOPE script's stages and materializes each once, re-optimized at the query optimizer's memo. The memo step is not portable, since managed warehouses do not expose their optimizer's internal state to external tools. We adapt the approach by detecting shared subexpressions at the AST level and selecting which to materialize heuristically. CSE is the strongest available position for a syntactic, cross-model approach on a managed warehouse.

\head{LearnedRewrite: improvements and regressions cancel}
LearnedRewrite applies one structural rule at scale on Tuva: it replaces \texttt{SELECT DISTINCT \ldots FROM $T_1$ JOIN $T_2$} with a join of two pre-deduplicated inputs. The rule fires on 42 \texttt{service\_category} models, which divide cleanly by join key. \texttt{service\_category\_\_home\_health\_professional} joins on the line-grain key \texttt{(claim\_id, claim\_line\_number)}: the pre-dedup \texttt{GROUP BY} is nearly free because line-grain keys are already close to unique, and shrinking the join inputs shrinks the join itself. Slot drops from 111\,s to 40\,s. \texttt{service\_category\_\_ambulance\_institutional} joins on \texttt{claim\_id} alone, which has many rows per value: the pre-dedup now requires a full \texttt{GROUP BY} of a wide table, and the resulting plan is worse than the broadcast hash join BigQuery would have chosen on the original. Slot rises from 135\,s to 211\,s. Across the 42 models, the line-grain wins and claim-grain losses nearly cancel. On the remaining ${\sim}180$ Tuva models the rule does not apply, and the per-execution slot is dominated by formatting-level differences (whitespace, identifier quoting, keyword case) that perturb BigQuery's plan cache. The headline of $-2.5$\% on single-run slot should not be over-interpreted: the project is essentially flat. The lesson is that the single rewrite rule that applies broadly on Tuva is, by construction, a wash, and rule-based per-query rewriting offers little headroom on this workload.

\head{GenRewrite: real per-model wins, bounded by per-model scope}
GenRewrite, an LLM-based per-query rewriter, finds optimizations LearnedRewrite cannot. We highlight three on Tuva. On \texttt{normalized\_input\_\_eligibility} (438\,s $\to$ 47\,s, $9.3\times$), the original joins a calendar reference table (covering decades of dates) against eligibility data through an inequality on dates, which BigQuery cannot push a filter through. GenRewrite computes the actual min/max enrollment dates in a separate CTE and uses them to filter the calendar before the inequality join. On \texttt{normalized\_input\_\_medical\_claim} (755\,s $\to$ 453\,s, $1.7\times$), the original enriches every line of the medical-claim table with ${\sim}12$ lookup joins. GenRewrite recognizes that roughly half of the lookups depend only on \texttt{claim\_id}, not on \texttt{claim\_line\_number}, and splits the enrichment so that claim-level lookups are performed once per distinct claim rather than once per line. On \texttt{acute\_inpatient\_\_generate\_encounter\_id} (168\,s $\to$ 55\,s, $3.1\times$), the original uses a self-join with a row-number inequality to compute claim merge reach in $O(n^2)$ per patient; GenRewrite replaces it with three single-pass aggregations and window functions, an $O(n)$ equivalent.

These are real semantic wins, none of which a syntactic rewriter could find. But every win is bounded to one model. GenRewrite cannot introduce a shared intermediate consumed by multiple models, cannot change a model's materialization, and cannot reduce the schedule of an over-scheduled model. It also has no mechanism for consistency across related models: the same encounter-merge rewrite applied successfully to \texttt{acute\_inpatient\_\_generate\_encounter\_id} above is, on the sibling \texttt{inpatient\_snf\_\_generate\_encounter\_id}, applied differently, with a worse outcome (159\,s $\to$ 355\,s). The headline 17.7\% on single-run slot is the ceiling of what per-model LLM rewriting can deliver here.

\head{CSE: subexpression sharing only}
CSE detects subexpressions shared across models at the AST level and extracts them into shared intermediates. Its single largest contribution on Tuva is the \texttt{service\_category} family, where 65 institutional/professional classifiers share enough syntactic structure that AST matching collapses the family from $6{,}135\,$s to $613\,$s. This is the regime CSE was built for. But its reach ends where AST matching stops. The 6 inpatient \texttt{*\_\_encounter\_grain} models compute the same family of per-encounter aggregations (paid/allowed/charge totals, claim counts, service-category flags, top diagnoses by paid amount) over different encounter types, with different upstream models, different filter values, and reference-table joins in different orders. The work is shared in substance but the SQL is not, and CSE's AST fingerprinting cannot match across them. CSE reduces the family by 14\% (914\,s $\to$ 787\,s) where \tool{} reduces it by 77\% (see below). On the project total, CSE reaches 34.6\% on single-run slot.

\head{\tool{}: closing the gap}
\tool{} reaches 51.1\% on single-run slot, $16.5$ points above CSE, and 67.7\% on frequency-weighted slot. Three mechanisms account for the gap, each grounded in a concrete refactoring chosen by the LLM stage and validated against the warehouse.

\emph{Cross-model factorization across dissimilar siblings.} The 6 inpatient \texttt{*\_\_encounter\_grain} models that CSE cannot match are exactly the targets of one of \tool{}'s refactorings. The LLM stage recognizes that the 6 models compute the same per-encounter aggregations and synthesizes a single shared intermediate that pre-aggregates them once across all encounter types, with \texttt{encounter\_type} carried as a column. Each consumer is rewritten to read pre-aggregated columns from the rollup, filter by its encounter type, and join the encounter-type-specific dimension tables. The 6 consumers drop from $914\,$s to $211\,$s at a $96\,$s build cost.

\emph{Cross-model column-projection pushdown.} The \texttt{combined\_claim\_line\_crosswalk} model is a 14-branch \texttt{UNION ALL}, each branch joining a different upstream model to the wide \texttt{stg\_medical\_claim} table to read a single column, \texttt{claim\_line\_number}. \tool{} extracts that column into a 21\,MB lookup table and rewrites all 14 branches to join the lookup instead. The model drops from $1{,}420\,$s to $374\,$s. CSE cannot do this because the 14 branches reference 14 different upstream models, so no AST subtree is shared across them.

\emph{Materialization tuning.} In the \texttt{normalized\_input} chain, six voting and normalize stages were materialized as tables in the original project (each ${\sim}70$--$125\,$s of build cost), feeding three \texttt{\_final} consumers. \tool{}'s materialization tuner flips the six stages to views: the upstream build cost drops to zero, the consumers absorb the inlined SQL, and the family's nine-model total drops from $544\,$s to $413\,$s. CSE's version of the same family regresses to $667\,$s, because CSE has no mechanism to flip materialization choices that the original project already committed to. This is the cleanest evidence in the data that materialization choices are coupled to the surrounding structure: the right materialization for the original project is not the right materialization for the refactored one.

The first two mechanisms account for the gap between single-run slot and CSE. The third partly does as well, and the further gap between single-run slot and frequency-weighted slot is contributed by the frequency layer, which reduces the schedule of over-scheduled models to the rate at which their inputs change. This third source of saving is invisible in the single-run column.

\subsection{Where the Gains Come From}
\label{sec:eval-attribution}

This subsection attributes \tool{}'s gains along two axes. We first ablate the three optimization dimensions to measure how much each contributes individually and in combination, then ablate the dataflow analysis to measure how much it contributes by targeting the LLM stage on the highest-payoff regions of the DAG.

Three observations follow.

\emph{Each dimension contributes independently.} Materialization tuning alone improves slot by 53.9\%, refactoring alone by 49.1\%, and frequency alone by 11.0\%. The original project is therefore sub-optimal along all three axes, not just one.

\emph{The dimensions are coupled.} R + M reaches 54.7\% slot, which exceeds both M alone (53.9\%) and R alone (49.1\%); the refactored DAG admits a different optimal materialization than the original DAG, and tuning the materialization for the refactored structure recovers additional savings that neither stage delivers in isolation. This matches the structural argument in Section~\ref{sec:approach} that refactoring and materialization decisions must be made jointly.

\emph{Frequency optimization is complementary to refactoring and materialization.} F alone delivers 11.0\% on slot; stacking F on top of R+M adds 13.0 points (67.7\% vs 54.7\%). The increment F contributes does not collapse when stacked on top of the other dimensions; over-scheduled models are over-scheduled regardless of how their code is refactored or how they are materialized.

\tool{} leads on every metric on both projects. On Tuva it reduces frequency-weighted slot by 67.7\%, almost 30 points beyond the strongest baseline. The three baselines fail in distinct ways, each consistent with a specific limitation of the technique. We walk through each on Tuva, then explain where \tool{}'s additional gains come from.

\head{LearnedRewrite: the headroom from per-query rewriting is small}
LearnedRewrite applies a portfolio of equivalence-preserving rules to each model's compiled SQL independently. On Tuva it nets $-2.5$\% slot. Inspection of the rewritten SQL shows that the one structural rule applied at scale converts \texttt{SELECT DISTINCT \ldots\ FROM $T_1$ JOIN $T_2$} into a join of two pre-deduplicated inputs. The rule is a win on models where the pre-dedup shrinks the join input enough to pay back its overhead, and a loss on models where BigQuery's planner would otherwise eliminate the join. The wins and losses roughly cancel. The lesson is not that LearnedRewrite is poorly designed but that per-query rule-based rewriting has little headroom on a project like Tuva: BigQuery's planner already handles most models well, and rules tuned for standalone analytical queries cannot exploit the cross-model structure that drives the remaining cost.

\head{GenRewrite: real per-model wins, but bounded by the per-model scope}
GenRewrite is an LLM-based per-query rewriter with verification, and it finds optimizations the rule-based baseline does not: per-model algorithmic rewrites such as predicate pushdown over inequality joins, functional-dependency-aware splitting of wide enrichment joins, and replacement of $O(n^2)$ self-joins with $O(n)$ window-function plans. These yield single-model speedups of $2$--$9\times$ where they apply. But GenRewrite operates one model at a time: it cannot introduce a shared intermediate that did not exist, change a model's materialization, or reduce the schedule of an over-scheduled model. Its 17.7\% Tuva slot reduction is the ceiling of what a per-model LLM rewriter can reach without crossing model boundaries.

\head{CSE: syntactic duplication only}
CSE detects subexpressions shared across models at the AST level and extracts them into shared intermediates. On Tuva it reduces slot by 34.6\%, the strongest baseline. Its largest contribution is the \texttt{service\_category} family, where 65 institutional/professional classifiers share enough syntactic structure that AST matching collapses the family from 6{,}135\,s to 613\,s. But CSE's reach ends where the AST stops matching. The \texttt{*\_\_encounter\_grain} family contains 25 models that compute the same family of per-encounter aggregations (paid/allowed/charge totals, service-category flags, top-by-paid-amount diagnoses and facilities) but over different encounter types, with different filter predicates and different join structures. The SQL is too syntactically dissimilar for AST fingerprinting to match across them, and CSE reduces the family's cost by only $9$\% ($5{,}056\,$s to $4{,}589\,$s). The same pattern explains CSE's negative result on Stripe: the project is already factored, so the duplicate subtrees that remain are small, and writing a new shared intermediate and scanning it from consumers costs more than the work it saves.

\head{Where \tool{}'s additional gains come from}
\tool{} closes the 30-point gap above CSE by exercising three mechanisms that a syntactic, single-dimension optimizer cannot.

\emph{Cross-model semantic factorization.} The 7 inpatient \texttt{*\_\_encounter\_grain} models that CSE refuses to merge are precisely the cases the LLM stage recognizes as semantically equivalent computations over different inputs. \tool{} synthesizes a shared rollup model that pre-aggregates the per-encounter measures once over the medical-claim source, and rewrites each consumer from a ${\sim}200$-line query into a ${\sim}70$-line lookup that joins the rollup against the encounter-type-specific dimension table. The 7 consumers drop from a combined $1{,}075\,$s to $309\,$s at a $96\,$s rollup-build cost. A second factorization addresses the \texttt{combined\_claim\_line\_crosswalk} model, whose 14-branch \texttt{UNION ALL} scans the wide \texttt{stg\_medical\_claim} table once per branch to read a single column; \tool{} extracts the narrow projection into a new map model, and the consumer drops from $1{,}420\,$s to $374\,$s. Neither factorization is reachable by AST matching: the consumers do not share syntax, and the new intermediates have no syntactic precedent in the original project.

\emph{Refactoring/materialization coupling.} Six models in the \texttt{normalized\_input} voting/normalize chain were materialized as tables in the original project. After \tool{} refactors their consumers, the materialization tuner flips them to views: the upstream tables are no longer worth their build cost given the new consumer structure, and inlining them is cheaper overall. The upstream cost drops by ${\sim}600\,$s; the downstream \texttt{\_final} consumers absorb ${\sim}360\,$s of additional work; the family nets ${\sim}240\,$s saved. CSE materializes everything, and its version of this family regresses by 22\%. The difference is direct evidence that materialization decisions depend on the refactored structure, not the original one, and that tuning them jointly is necessary to capture the saving.

\emph{Frequency-aware scheduling.} Independent of any structural change, models whose declared schedule fires faster than their inputs change are over-scheduled. \tool{}'s frequency layer detects these and reduces their schedules to match the rate at which their data actually changes, eliminating redundant executions outright. This mechanism is invisible at the single-run slot metric but contributes the remaining gap to the frequency-weighted column.

In aggregate on Tuva, \tool{} introduces 14 new shared models costing $514\,$s in build time and recovers $9{,}092\,$s on the consumers they serve. CSE introduces 1 new model. The contrast in investment reflects the contrast in scope: a syntactic baseline can only commit to new intermediates it can prove duplicated at the AST level, while the LLM-driven candidate generator commits to intermediates justified by semantic equivalence and validated against the warehouse.

\section{Evaluation Plan}
\label{sec:eval}

This draft currently focuses on problem formulation and system design. The final version should evaluate \tool along four questions: whether it reduces end-to-end warehouse cost, which classes of dependency-aware refactorings account for the gains, how often the verification loop rejects unsafe rewrites, and how the benefits vary with workload frequency skew and DAG size.

\subsection{Workloads and Baselines}

The evaluation should include at least one real dbt project, one SQLMesh- or warehouse-task-style pipeline when available, and semi-synthetic benchmarks derived from recurring analytics pipelines. Natural baselines include the original DAG, materialization-only tuning, schedule-only tuning, structural analyses without LLM refactoring, and single-query rewrite systems that do not cross node boundaries.

\subsection{Metrics}

Primary metrics should include total slot time or warehouse cost over a fixed horizon, refresh latency at the DAG sinks, the number of nodes changed, and verification pass rate. Secondary diagnostics should measure optimizer runtime, LLM usage, and the distribution of wins across semantic reuse, contract-aware rewriting, computation placement, materialization coupling, and freshness-driven restructuring.

\subsection{Ablations}

The final paper should isolate the contribution of each layer: candidate identification, proposal filtering, equivalence diagnostics, materialization tuning, and frequency-aware reasoning. A particularly important ablation is whether frequency-aware splitting produces gains that materialization-only optimization cannot recover.
